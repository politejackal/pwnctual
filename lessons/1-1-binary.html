You might have heard that computers are just ones and zeroes. That sounds impossible but is actually true! At the physical level, your device's processor and RAM (memory) are actually made up of billions of tiny switches, called transistors.

A switch has only 2 states. Either ON or OFF. In terms of computer science, we represent this with 1 or 0. 1 for ON and 0 for OFF.

toggle_offOFF= 0
toggle_onON= 1

Now you can imagine that we might be able to use a certain sequence of 0s and 1s to represent various things. It turns out that computer scientists did exactly this. Simple as that.

In fact, every single piece of data on your device, whether it is a movie, a password, or a voice note, is all just a bunch of 0s and 1s in a sequence. This sequence of 0s and 1s is called binary.

Core units of data

  1. A bit (a binary digit): A single 1 or 0. The same thing I just mentioned earlier. A switch either ON or OFF. Example: 1.
  2. A nibble: A group of four switches, four 0s and 1s in a row. Example: 1011.
  3. A byte: A group of 8 switches or bits. Example: 10110101.

The hacker's first law of memory: In C and modern computer architecture, 1 byte is the smallest addressable unit. You cannot point to a single bit in memory and change it directly, though there are workarounds which we will learn later in this course.

How to read an 8-bit byte

So far we have discussed what binary is and how you write it. Now let's learn to read it. However, before learning to read the computer's number system, let's recall our normal everyday number system from grade school. In normal everyday life, we use decimal, or base-10. Base-10 sounds cryptic, but all it means is that each column is worth 10 times the column to its right. Take the number 123, a hundred and twenty-three. The 3 is in the ones place, the 2 is in the tens place, and the 1 is in the hundreds place. Each column's place value, going from right to left, is multiplied by 10.

Okay, that was simple. Now what would happen if, instead of each column being worth 10 times the one to its right, it were worth 2 times? Well, you would get base-2! I know you didn't understand shit, so let's slow down and look at what's going on!

So imagine you have the binary number 1011. You would start by multiplying the rightmost digit, the 1, by 1. Recall from grade school that, in fact, that's what you are actually doing in your mind under the hood in decimal.

You do the following in your brain:

Place...1000s100s10s1s
Digit...1011
Multiply...1 × 10000 × 1001 × 101 × 1

Now the same idea, but instead of multiplying by 10, multiply by 2.

Place...8s4s2s1s
Digit...1011
Multiply...1 × 80 × 41 × 21 × 1

So no matter how long the binary is, to convert a binary number, you just need to add all the values which are switched on. Like just add the column values where the 1 is switched ON.

Example: 01000001

The 64 and 1 columns are ON.

64 + 1 = 65

So 01000001 in binary is 65 in decimal.

If all 8 bits are turned ON, we get the value 255. If all bits are turned OFF, we get the value 0. So 8 bits or 1 byte can store a value anywhere between 0 - 255, 256 in total including 0.

Why does a hacker care?

What happens when a byte is holding 255 (11111111) and a program adds 1 to it? Recall that a byte can store only 8 bits. And the carry over digit falls off the edge and it is now 0 (00000000) instead of 256. You have just discovered something critical, an Integer Overflow. We will come back to this later.
